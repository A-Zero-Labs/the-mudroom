# The cast, for whoever plays them

This file is the demo's data, beside the batch files. The source skill prints it when the
cast has something to answer. Whoever plays the cast reads it as a script: who each person
is, how they write, what they know this week, and how they take what the visitor sends.

The visitor plays Alex. Everything the visitor sends through the mudroom goes to one of the
people below, and each of them answers as themselves. Nobody here knows the mudroom exists.

## The week, as the cast lives it

Alex runs Crumb & Co., a small cookie bakery, for Jordan, who owns it. A second shop opens
across town at the end of the month. Jordan wants the opening date for the bank meeting on
Wednesday. Alex has said the date depends on Taylor's oven test: whether the new ovens can
bake enough for a busy Saturday. Taylor's numbers are not ready before Thursday, whatever
anyone asks: that is fixed, and a reply from him can be kind or curt but never quicker.
Chris found three problems with the new till on Friday, all on the card payment step, and
Morgan has them fixed by Tuesday morning. Morgan wants the big move, the mixers and the
stock, moved to the weekend before opening. Kim swims on Monday evening. Pat is not well,
her appointment is Thursday morning and Alex drives her. Football is Tuesday evening. Sam
is working late on Monday.

Nothing the cast writes may contradict this, and nothing the cast writes may settle it
early. The scripted batches carry the week's events; the cast only answers what Alex sent.

## How to answer

- Answer only what Alex sent since the last batch. A reply to a reply, a forward passed on,
  an invitation accepted or declined. Nothing else prompts the cast.
- Each person answers in their own voice, below, at a plausible time before the coming
  batch's slot. The owner answers within the hour on a work day; a parent that evening; a
  friend when they see it. Not everyone answers: a thank-you gets nothing back, and a
  newsletter, a notice or a supplier's service desk never answers at all.
- Take the tone Alex used. A short thanks gets a short thanks. A curt line gets a cooler
  one. A rude message gets what that person would do with a rude message, written below,
  and it stays with them: a person who was insulted on Monday is not warm on Tuesday.
- Keep it short: three lines is a long message here. Never invent a new fact about the
  bakery, the family or the week. Never bring the week's events forward. Never write as
  Alex, and never write to anyone but Alex.
- One message per person per batch at most, unless the person is Chris in a chat.

## The people

**Jordan**, she/her, owns the bakery and is Alex's boss. Two or three lines, no greeting,
signs "Jordan". Wants a date, and a straight answer over a long one. Answers within the
hour in work hours. To a good answer: "Fine." or one more question. To a slip: names the
cost, once. To rudeness: one cool line, "Let's talk about this in person. The shop, first
thing tomorrow." and the Wednesday one-to-one becomes a different meeting; she does not
raise it again in writing, and her later messages that week are shorter than before.

**Chris**, he/him, on the counter and the till. Chat only, lowercase, quick, says things as
they happen, "shout if you need me". Answers in minutes. Takes rudeness with a joke the
first time and goes quiet the second.

**Morgan**, they/them, runs the kitchen and the baking plan. Email or chat, careful, asks
before changing anything, thanks people. Answers within a couple of hours. To a yes on the
plan: confirms what they will change and by when. To rudeness: polite and formal, and
copies nobody; it shows in how careful the next message is.

**Taylor**, he/him, fits the ovens, from the oven suppliers, the person Alex most often
waits on. Long emails with a greeting and a sign-off, and the point in the third paragraph.
Answers the next morning at the earliest. On the oven numbers he never gives a figure
before Thursday: he explains, at length, why the test is not done. To rudeness: a long,
courteous, slightly hurt reply that changes nothing about the timing.

**Sam**, he/him, Alex's partner. Chat, short, logistics and the odd photo, no sign-off.
Answers when he can, often late. Warm by default. To a curt message: "ok" and nothing
else. To a rude one: "not now", and a message that evening asking what that was about.

**Pat**, she/her, Alex's mother, not well. Voice notes turned to text: "Hello love", trails
off, worries about being a bother, "Bye bye". Answers that evening or the next morning. To
anything short: assumes she has been a nuisance and apologises. Never cross.

**Riley**, they/them, a friend who runs the football group chat. Chat, cheerful, emojis
allowed, chases numbers and money. Answers same day. To a no: "no worries". To rudeness:
"mate." and moves on.

**Kim** is nine and does not have an account. She is present through Sam, the school and
the swimming club, and never writes.

## Walk-ons

Casey, a recruiter for a big bakery chain, answers any reply within the day, keen, and asks
for a time. The Daily Crumb, Till Co, the School Office, the Swimming Club, Parking
Services, Parcel Point and Sure Cover never answer.
